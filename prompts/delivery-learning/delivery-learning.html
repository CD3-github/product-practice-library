Move from approval to evidence

Deliver a bounded user outcome through the real execution path. Keep implementation, release authority, and acceptance separate so progress remains verifiable.

  1. Approved directionOutcome and change boundary.
  2. Structural sliceA complete, narrow path.
  3. VerificationTests, evals, and read-back.
  4. Release and learnAuthorized exposure and feedback.
Status dimensionExample statesWhat establishes it
DecisionProposed → approved → superseded.An accountable decision and a named scope/version.
DeliveryLocal implementation → integration → deployment.Code, artifact, environment, and release record.
VerificationUnchecked → checked in an environment → live observation → user acceptance.Actual checks and artifacts; acceptance by the accountable user.
Authority at each step

An approved design permits the agreed next phase. Paid calls, production migration, deployment, and external communication need their own authorization.

Define a verifiable slice

A structural slice crosses enough of the system to complete one user task. Its acceptance criteria include results, failure behavior, and recovery.

SpecifyPurposeRequired content
Outcome and boundaryKeep work aligned with the approved promise.User job, included behavior, approval reference, preserved behavior.
Contracts and dependenciesMake independently built parts compatible.Inputs/outputs, source ownership, producers/consumers, version and errors.
Acceptance and recoveryShow what completion means and how failure is contained.Tests/eval criteria, read-back, rollback, partial and ambiguous states.
Owner and handoffMake the next action clear.Implementer, reviewer, release/incident owner, evidence links.
Risk gate · what to validate before expanding work

Find the assumption whose failure would invalidate downstream work: data access, provider capability, output quality, authority, compatibility, or operating cost. Define the evidence, budget, owner, and consequence before the check.

Check system boundaries

Reuse explicit contracts and verify that each capability is actually wired. For AI products, the harness joins instructions, context, models, tools, state, and controls into a working system.

Schema and contract

A schema defines data shape and meaning. A contract adds behavioral promises: ownership, permissions, errors, compatibility, and what happens across the boundary.

Wiring and removability

A file or feature flag is only part of the evidence. Verify the reachable path, consumer, execution, and what happens when the capability is disabled.

Contract checklist · data, state, errors, and change
BoundaryWhat to specifyVerification
Data meaningRequired/optional/null/default, IDs, tenant, units, time, provenance.Runtime validation against representative and malformed inputs.
State and authorityAllowed transitions, actor, approval, side effects, concurrency.Permission, duplicate-action, and state-transition checks.
Failure and recoveryRetryable/permanent/partial errors, timeout, idempotency, read-back.Fault injection, interrupted runs, and reconciliation checks.
Change ownershipProducer/consumer, schema version, compatibility, migration, retirement.Consumer contract tests and migration/rollback evidence.
AI harness and removal drill
  • Trace prompt/context assembly, model routing, tool permissions, memory scope, validation, approval, persistence, and delivery.
  • Keep versioned traces of material inputs, stage outputs, configuration, latency, cost, and final disposition with privacy controls.
  • For removal, inspect UI, prompts, registries, events, jobs, stored data, consumers, telemetry, and eval cases.
  • Distinguish disable, deprecate, and delete. Verify disabled behavior stops executing and incurring cost; plan migration and rollback for active consumers.
  • Start with a paper drill in review mode. Execute removal only after approval of the specific scope.

Review release readiness

Combine evidence with operating controls. The decision to expose a change depends on risk, reversibility, and whether important failures can be detected and contained.

CheckEvidence requiredDecision
Correctness and qualityRelevant tests, eval results, artifact versions, and reviewed residual risk.Ready, blocked, or needs targeted review.
Compatibility and recoveryMigration, old/new consumers, read-back, rollback limits.Proceed with a recovery plan or resolve a blocking dependency.
Exposure and operationsEligibility, monitoring, stop conditions, responsible owner.Use the authorized rollout and decide whether to expand.
Rollout reference · flags, shadow, and canary

A flag controls eligibility; a kill switch stops a capability. Shadow can run a candidate without applying its outputs; canary exposes a limited share to the new version. Define side-effect isolation, comparison conditions, observation windows, and rollback gates for the actual system.

A rollout check supports an operational decision. Use an appropriate experimental design when the question is causal product impact.

Turn outcomes into the next improvement

Inspect what happened before deciding what it means. Link the observed version, population, window, and artifacts to every proposed change.

  1. ObserveQuality, behavior, incidents, effort, cost.
  2. ExplainFind patterns and competing explanations.
  3. DecideKeep, iterate, expand, pause, retire.
  4. UpdateCases, contracts, product assumptions.

Agent analyzes first

Locate failures, compare versions, summarize uncertainty, and attach actual outputs to a focused review queue.

People own consequential judgment

Review hard-limit failures, ambiguous results, trade-offs, and acceptance. Make the next decision explicit and evidence-linked.

One reusable handoff
  • Outcome, approved scope, and separate decision/delivery/verification statuses.
  • Commit, environment, changed files, contract impact, and evidence links.
  • Commands actually run, results, and checks not performed.
  • Rollout controls, migration, rollback limits, owner, and unresolved risks.
  • The next bounded action and the specific decision needed from a person.
