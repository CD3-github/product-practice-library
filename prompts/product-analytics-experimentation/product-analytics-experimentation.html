Measurement foundation

Understand how the numbers are produced

A product metric connects three things: who used the product, what they did, and how the result is counted. Check that chain before treating a change in the chart as a change in the product.

01 · Identity

Who are we counting?

Recognize the same person across visits or devices; use an account as the unit when the product serves a team.

Example: one person using a phone and a laptop still counts as one user.

02 · Events

What actually happened?

An event records an action at a defined moment. A click on Save and a successful save are different events.

Example: count success only after the document has been saved.

03 · Metrics

How is the result counted?

Turn events into a number using a consistent population, formula, and time window.

Example: of 100 users who started a draft this week, 40 saved it within seven days: 40% completion.

Then interpret the result

Compare periods or cohorts (groups defined by a shared condition) using the same metric definition, then decide what to investigate or improve.

Why verify this first? A broken success event can make completion appear to fall. Duplicate events can inflate an event-count metric. Both can change the chart while the actual experience stays the same.
Implementation details: what to check at each step

Identity — avoid splitting or merging people incorrectly

Link anonymous activity to the correct signed-in user. Define how multiple people belong to a shared account or workspace, and how deletion affects reporting.

Events — make the record match the action

Verify the trigger, required information, success/failure distinction, timestamps, and duplicate handling. Compare a few known actions with their stored records.

Metrics — make comparisons use the same definition

Check the counted unit, numerator, denominator, time window, and exclusions. Confirm the dashboard and source query return the same result.

A measurement contract is the written agreement for these definitions: what counts, how it is recorded, how it is calculated, and which version the analysis uses.

Cohort, time cohort, and experiment assignment

Cohort · a group defined by a shared condition

For example, users who signed up in the same week, or users who saved at least one draft last month. Reuse the definition when comparing behavior or tracking return visits.

Time cohort · grouped by a shared starting period

For example, compare users who signed up in week 1 and week 2 at the same point after signup. This makes retention comparisons fairer.

Experiment assignment · deciding who receives A or B

Among eligible participants, assign users or accounts to variants according to the experiment design. Bucketing is a technical mechanism for making that assignment repeatable; with stable inputs and configuration, the same unit keeps its group.

Example: users who signed up this week→ Select this cohort as the eligible population→ Randomly assign participants to A or B→ Record which experience each participant actually encountered

If the treatment can change cohort membership, comparing only members after the experiment can bias the result. Define the experiment population and analysis plan before launch.

In an experiment: assignment and exposure are separate records

Assignment means the system selected a variant for someone. Exposure means they reached the defined point where that variant could affect their experience. A user assigned to a new editor may never open it.

Keep both records. The primary analysis often follows the original assignment; an exposed-user analysis needs a pre-defined rule because opening the feature may itself be affected by the treatment.

Behavior analysis

Use the workflow to locate the question

These views answer different questions about the same experience. Start with the view closest to the product decision.

Start

Adoption + activation

Adoption asks who starts using the feature. Activation asks who reaches its first meaningful result, such as saving a usable draft.

Complete

Funnel

Track how many people reach each step: open editor → generate → save. A large drop identifies a step to investigate.

Return

Retention + cohort

Follow a defined group over time. For example, how many of this week’s new users return to complete a task next week?

Effort

Journey + effort

Inspect the route to completion: how often do people retry, edit, or backtrack, and how long does it take?

Investigate

Choose the next evidence

Use the pattern to decide whether to interview users, inspect quality, repair data, or test a product change.

Example: many users generate a draft, but few save it

First confirm the save event is recorded correctly. Then locate the drop between generation and saving, and compare new and returning users using consistent definitions.

Several explanations remain possible: weak output, a hard-to-find save button, or no intention to save. Review sample outputs and user sessions or interview users before selecting a change to test.

A funnel locates the drop; it does not identify the cause on its own. A controlled experiment can then estimate whether the proposed change improves completion.

Controlled experimentation

Plan how to tell whether a change helps

An A/B experiment randomly assigns eligible participants to two experiences and compares the results. For example, test whether a clearer save button improves draft completion while error rate stays acceptable.

01 · Plan

What changes, and for whom?

State the hypothesis, define eligible participants, and assign them to the current experience A or changed experience B.

Design details: population and assignment

Choose an assignment unit: a person, account, or workspace. Keep a whole team in one group when teammates could affect one another.

Record the intended difference between A and B, the proposed mechanism, and actual exposure. Check that participants do not accidentally receive both variants.

02 · Measure

What improvement matters?

Choose one main outcome, such as task completion, and guardrails for things that must remain acceptable, such as errors or cost.

Design details: effect size and sample

Baseline is the current level. Practical significance is the smallest improvement worth the effort. MDE is the effect size the experiment is designed to detect with its chosen sample and statistical power.

Statistical power describes the chance of detecting an effect of that size if it exists. Estimate sample size and duration from the baseline, variation, MDE, and chosen error rates.

03 · Decide

When is there enough evidence?

Agree on duration, analysis, and stopping rules in advance. Review the estimated effect and its uncertainty before expanding or revising the change.

Analysis details: validity and uncertainty

SRM (sample-ratio mismatch) means the observed group sizes differ unexpectedly from the planned split. Investigate assignment or logging problems before interpreting impact.

An uncertainty interval shows how precise the effect estimate is. Check guardrails, respect the planned stopping rule, and label unplanned subgroup analyses as exploratory.

When A/A, canary, or shadow is useful

A/A · check the experiment setup

Give both groups the same experience to check randomization, records, and analysis. One non-significant result alone does not prove the setup is reliable.

Canary · release to a small share first

Watch errors, quality, and other limits while limiting exposure. Expand gradually or roll back; causal attribution still requires an appropriate experiment design.

Shadow · run alongside the live version

Process mirrored real inputs without serving the candidate result or executing its external actions. Compare outputs safely before giving the candidate production responsibility.

Connected evidence

AI quality and product outcomes meet at the decision

A better offline score does not automatically improve user behavior, and higher usage does not prove output quality. Preserve each claim type, then join them with shared release, variant, and trace identifiers.
